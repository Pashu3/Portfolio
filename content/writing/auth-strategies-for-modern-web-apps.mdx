Auth is the thing every project does, half of them do badly, and most teams never revisit. Picking the right primitive at the start saves a rewrite later.

## Session cookies — the boring choice

A session ID stored in an HTTP-only cookie, mapped to a row in a sessions table. Old, simple, hard to get wrong. Logout is a real DELETE. Revocation is a real DELETE. Most apps should start here.

The complaint people raise is "doesn't scale" — which mostly translates to "I'd need a session store." You probably already have Redis or Postgres. The session store is one of those.

## JWTs — popular, often wrong

JWTs are stateless tokens. Logout becomes an exercise in "wait for it to expire" or "maintain a denylist that defeats the point of being stateless." For internal service-to-service calls, fine. For user sessions, the operational cost almost always outweighs the supposed scaling benefit.

## OAuth — necessary, hard to do well

If you're letting users sign in with Google, GitHub, or Apple, you're doing OAuth. Use a battle-tested library. Don't roll your own. The flows are simple to demo and full of edge cases — refresh tokens, scope changes, account linking, the user revoking your app outside your UI. The library handles 90% of these correctly. You'll handle 50% if you write it yourself.

## Magic links — UX win, security gotchas

Send a one-time link to email, click, signed in. Lovely UX. Two failure modes to plan for: emails sometimes take minutes to arrive, and tokens that don't expire are tokens an attacker can use. Short TTLs and single-use are non-negotiable.

## Picking by use case

- B2C consumer app, password optional → OAuth + magic links.
- B2B SaaS with admin controls → OAuth + session cookies, scoped per-tenant.
- Internal tools → SSO, session cookies, no exceptions.
- Service-to-service → signed JWTs with short TTLs, separate from user auth.

> Auth is the kind of thing where "simple and boring" beats "clever and stateless" nine times out of ten.
